About

The short version

I started out as a product developer — five years of SolidWorks, prototypes, and learning how things actually get built. That background never left: as a project manager and Release Train Engineer, I'm the person who can sit in an engineering discussion and follow the argument, then walk into a steering meeting and translate it.

My day job is coordination at scale: PI planning, release schedules, dependencies between agile teams, and clearing whatever is in their way. My evenings often look surprisingly similar — except the teams are replaced by containers, coding agents, and a soldering iron.

I care about two things in delivery: honesty in status and momentum. Plans that reflect reality, risks that get named early, and teams that can focus because someone keeps the noise away from them.

AI experience

I don't just use AI — I build with it

AI is part of how I work every day, at the office and at home. Not as a buzzword, but as a set of tools I've learned to steer: where they're brilliant, where they quietly fail, and how to keep a human accountable for the result.

⌨️

Agentic development

Most of my side projects — this website, four iOS apps, several self-hosted tools — were built in close collaboration with AI coding agents. I plan, review, and decide; the agents write and iterate. That workflow taught me more about the real capabilities and limits of LLMs than any course could.

🔗

AI in enterprise workflows

I've built assistants that read, analyze, and update content in enterprise tools like Confluence and Jira in controlled, reviewable workflows — plus automations with n8n that connect AI to everyday processes. Practical experience with what it takes to make AI useful inside a company.

🖥️

Self-hosted & local models

I run open models on my own hardware and evaluate them against commercial ones. That means first-hand experience with the privacy, cost, and capability trade-offs organizations face when they decide how to adopt AI.

⚖️

Judgment stays human

My rule for AI at work is simple: it can accelerate research, drafting, and iteration — but ownership, review, and accountability never move to the machine. That's also how I'd introduce it to a team.
